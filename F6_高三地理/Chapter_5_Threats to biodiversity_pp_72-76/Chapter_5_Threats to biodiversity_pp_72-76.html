Case Study A • Urbanisation, Shanty Towns & Cattle Deforestation (pp.73–74)

🇧🇷 Brazil: Manaus () & The Cattle-Soy Frontier

🗺️ Google Maps — Manaus (Amazonas) & Porto Velho Highway Corridor
📍 1. Geographic Location & Case Foreword (地理位置與案例前言):

Manaus (3.1°S, 60.0°W) is the capital city of the state of Amazonas in northwestern Brazil, situated at the iconic confluence of the dark-water Rio Negro and the sandy-colored Amazon River (Solimões) in the heart of the world’s largest tropical rainforest (瑪瑙斯位於巴西西北部亞馬遜州核心［3.1°S, 60.0°W］，坐落於黑河［Rio Negro］與亞馬遜河著名的「兩河交匯處」).

🔬 2. Case Situation & Key Textbook Data (課本 pp.73–74 關鍵數據與威脅機制):

• Industrialisation & Demographic Boom (p.73): Famous for the late 19th/early 20th-century rubber boom, Manaus established the Manaus Free Trade Zone (Zona Franca) in 1967 (chemical, automotive, electronic factories). Population surged from 90,000 in 1950 to 2.3 million in 2023 (growth rate 10% higher than other Brazilian cities).
• Shanty Towns & Waste Flooding (p.73): Rapid migration led to shanty towns like Monte Horebe (5,000 residents clearing rainforest with chainsaws and fire), with new occupation attempts occurring once every two weeks. Poor waste disposal blocks rivers and causes rainy-season flooding. Reopening the disused 870 km highway to Porto Velho (closed since 1988) threatens further encroachment.
• Cattle & Soy Frontier (pp.73–74): Across the wider Brazilian Amazon (Figure 5.11), cattle ranching accounts for 80% of rainforest clearance, releasing 340M tonnes of carbon/yr (3.4% global emissions) and removing 800 million trees in Brazil between 2017 and 2022.

🇺🇸 USA (Yellowstone National Park): , &

🗺️ Google Maps — Yellowstone National Park (Wyoming, Montana, Idaho, USA)
📍 1. Geographic Location & Case Foreword (地理位置與案例前言):

Located primarily in northwestern Wyoming (extending into Montana and Idaho, 44.6°N, 110.5°W) in the Western United States, Yellowstone National Park (designated in 1872) is a temperate montane ecosystem of geothermal basins, mountains, meadows, rivers, and forests (黃石國家公園位於美國西部懷俄明州、蒙大拿州與愛達荷州交界處［44.6°N, 110.5°W］，於 1872 年設立，為涵蓋地熱盆地、高山、草甸與森林的溫帶生態系統).

🔬 2. Case Situation & Trophic Cascade Timeline (課本 pp.76–77 關鍵時間軸與級聯機制):

• 1872–1924 Eradication: Fearing wolf predation on elk and bison herds, local, state, and federal governments ran an eradication programme; the last remaining wolf pups were killed in 1924.
• Top-Down Trophic Cascade & River Erosion (1924–1995): Lacking an apex , populations exploded and overgrazed grasses, sedges, and reeds—harming fish and . Without plant roots to bind soil, stream banks eroded, and removing shade shrubs caused lake and river temperatures to increase.
• 1995 Reintroduction to January 2023 Recovery: Wolves were reintroduced in 1995; elk numbers shrank to natural levels, willow/reed banks and beaver colonies recovered, and as of January 2023, there were 108 wolves in the park, divided into ten packs.

✍️ Model Paper 3 Essay Response (Level 3 Full 10 Marks 滿分範文):

The Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) model is a useful conceptual starting point for explaining how early-stage economic development drives severe ecosystem destruction. According to the EKC (Figure 5.12), environmental degradation intensifies during initial industrialisation due to limited conservation funding and low environmental awareness. This rising limb accurately reflects Resource 1A in the Brazilian Amazon: following the establishment of the Manaus Free Trade Zone (Zona Franca) in 1967 for chemical, automotive, and electronic manufacturing, Manaus’s population surged from 90,000 in 1950 to 2.3 million in 2023 (growing 10% faster than other Brazilian cities). This rapid urban growth triggered informal shanty-town clearing—such as Monte Horebe (5,000 residents), with new occupation attempts occurring every two weeks—and severe river waste blockage. Simultaneously, export-driven farm expansion across the Amazon (Figure 5.11) sees slash-and-burn cattle ranching account for 80% of rainforest clearance (releasing 340 million tonnes of carbon annually, or 3.4% of global emissions) and the removal of 800 million trees in Brazil between 2017 and 2022 as soy farmers displace pastures deeper into pioneer forest.

Furthermore, the EKC’s turning point and downward limb are partially supported by empirical evidence from the PRC between 2004 and 2013 (Resource 1B). After initial industrial degradation, most pollutants in the PRC reached a turning point at around US $8,000 per capita. As national wealth and technological capacity grew, the PRC invested nearly US $900 billion in renewable power and fuels since 2009 (more than double the investment of the USA), driving the dramatic surge in renewable electricity generation between 2000 and 2020 shown in Figure 5.13.

However, when regarded critically, the EKC model is overly simplistic and has severe limitations. First, different pollutants have vastly different thresholds: in the PRC, SO₂ emissions required over US $100,000 per capita to turn around, and Figure 5.13 shows that total coal generation still increased alongside renewables between 2000 and 2020. Second, historical data from 1965 to 1990 show that East Asian and PRC economic growth reduced poverty and lowered inequality (contradicting Kuznets’ assumption), while green technologies were only implemented in 2003—a time lag of over a decade. Third, in reality, pollution often continues to rise in low-income economies while high-income consumption exacerbates global climate change, producing an augmented Kuznets curve (Activity 4) with limited predictive ability. Most importantly for Topic A2, while air pollution can be cleaned up after reaching an income threshold, tropical rainforest clearance and species extinction are irreversible—meaning the EKC is only useful if treated critically as a simplified model rather than an automatic guarantee of ecosystem recovery.

✍️ Model Paper 3 Essay Response (Level 3 Full 10 Marks 滿分範文):

Financial gains and economic incentives play a decisive role in shaping local and community attitudes towards conservation, particularly in developing biomes where livelihoods depend directly on land resources. When forests yield no local income, they are frequently cleared for agriculture or pasture (such as the 80% of rainforest clearance driven by cattle ranching in Brazil). However, as Resource 2B demonstrates, can transform local attitudes by making intact ecosystems more profitable than cleared land. In the Napo region of the Ecuadorian Amazon (Figure 5.8), around 10,000 ecotourists per year generate US $12 million for the local economy by employing guides, hospitality workers, and local artisans. Similarly, in Brazil’s Pantanal, land acquired by Conservation International for ecotourism generates higher revenue per acre than surrounding cattle ranches, injecting foreign revenue directly into conservation. Nevertheless, financial gains only foster positive attitudes if they are distributed fairly; if local communities and Indigenous peoples are excluded from tourism profits or banned from sustainable hunting, fishing, and timber harvesting, severe conflicts arise between conservation goals and local economic survival.

On the other hand, perceptions of ecological value—centred on keystone and iconic species—are equally vital, particularly in shifting government policy, scientific management, and international public support. A keystone species exerts a disproportionately large impact on ecosystem structure relative to its abundance. As Resource 2A shows, when Yellowstone National Park was designated in 1872, negative economic perceptions led governments to eradicate grey wolves (killing the last pups in 1924) to protect elk and bison. This triggered a catastrophic top-down trophic cascade: elk populations exploded and overgrazed grasses, sedges, and reeds, decimating beaver and fish populations, eroding stream banks due to lost root binding, and warming rivers as shade shrubs disappeared. It was scientific recognition of the wolf’s keystone ecological role—not direct financial profit—that drove the government to reintroduce wolves in 1995, successfully restoring the ecosystem (reaching 108 wolves in 10 packs by January 2023). Meanwhile, iconic species—such as the orangutan, three-toed sloth, poison-dart frog, and jaguar in tropical rainforests—shape public attitudes by building cultural and emotional connections that raise global conservation awareness.

In conclusion, financial gains and ecological roles are mutually dependent rather than mutually exclusive. While recognising the ecological role of keystone species is paramount for scientific policy (as in Yellowstone), and iconic species generate the emotional appeal that attracts visitors and donors, equitable financial gains from ecotourism and sustainable resource use are ultimately essential on the ground to ensure that local and Indigenous communities value and protect biodiversity long-term.
